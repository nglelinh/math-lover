## Hook

Two friends first visit the library on the same day. One comes back every 4 days. The other comes back every 6 days.

When will they meet again at the library?

You are looking for the *smallest* number that is a multiple of both 4 and 6.

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

- explain what a least common multiple (LCM) is
- find the LCM of two small numbers by listing multiples
- find the LCM using prime bricks
- use LCM to solve “when do they meet again?” stories

## What We Already Know

You already know how to list multiples and how to find a GCF.

Today we flip the question. Instead of “largest number that divides both,” we ask “smallest number that both divide.”

## Discovery Puzzle

Write the first eight multiples of 4.

Write the first eight multiples of 6.

Circle the numbers that appear in both lists.

Which circled number is the smallest (not counting 0)?

That smallest shared multiple is the next library day.

## Core Idea

The **least common multiple** (LCM) of two numbers is the smallest positive whole number that both numbers divide exactly.

### Method 1: List and choose

LCM of 4 and 6

**Step 1.** Multiples of 4: $$4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, \ldots$$

Multiples of 6: $$6, 12, 18, 24, 30, \ldots$$

**Step 2.** Shared multiples: $$12, 24, \ldots$$

**Step 3.** Smallest shared positive multiple:

$$\mathrm{LCM}(4, 6) = 12$$

They meet again after 12 days.

### Method 2: All the bricks, using the larger count

**Step 1.** Break each number into primes.

$$4 = 2 \times 2$$

$$6 = 2 \times 3$$

**Step 2.** For every prime that appears in either number, keep the *larger* count.

Two 2s (from 4) and one 3 (from 6).

$$\mathrm{LCM} = 2 \times 2 \times 3 = 12$$

Compare with GCF: GCF keeps the *smaller* shared count. LCM keeps the *larger* overall count. That is why the two ideas are partners.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Three quick LCMs

- 3 and 5 → LCM = 15
- 4 and 7 → LCM = 28
- 6 and 8 → LCM = 24

For 3 and 5, notice they share no prime bricks, so the LCM is just their product.

### Activity 2: Library day

Friend A every 4 days. Friend B every 6 days. First visit together today.

$$\mathrm{LCM}(4, 6) = 12$$

They meet again in 12 days.

### Activity 3: Fast bricks

LCM of 15 and 20

$$15 = 3 \times 5$$

$$20 = 2 \times 2 \times 5$$

Keep two 2s, one 3, and one 5.

$$\mathrm{LCM} = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 = 60$$

## Example Walkthrough

$$\mathrm{LCM}(5, 7)$$

Neither shares a factor besides 1, so $$\mathrm{LCM} = 5 \times 7 = 35$$.

$$\mathrm{LCM}(8, 12)$$

Multiples of 8: $$8, 16, 24, 32, \ldots$$

Multiples of 12: $$12, 24, 36, \ldots$$

Shared smallest: 24.

$$\mathrm{LCM}(3, 4) = 12$$, not 7 (adding is the wrong tool) and not 24 (that is a common multiple, but not the least).

## Math Through Time

Calendar makers needed a number that is a multiple of both a “moon month” and a “sun year.” That is LCM thinking, even when the real lengths are not tidy whole numbers.

Drummers meet LCM when one pattern lasts 3 beats and another lasts 4: they line up again after 12 beats.

Computers use LCM when two clocks must tick together after different step sizes.

## Challenge Ladder

Level 1: Find $$\mathrm{LCM}(5, 7)$$, $$\mathrm{LCM}(8, 12)$$, and $$\mathrm{LCM}(10, 15)$$.

Level 2: Two buses leave together. One returns every 8 minutes. The other returns every 12 minutes. When do they leave together again?

Level 3: The LCM of 3 and 4 is which of these: 7, 12, or 24? Why do people sometimes pick 7?

Level 4: For 4 and 6 we found GCF = 2 and LCM = 12. Compute $$4 \times 6$$ and $$2 \times 12$$. What do you notice? Test the same pattern on 8 and 12.

## Parent Corner

The child is learning to find the *next time two repeating schedules line up*.

Helpful prompts:

- "Write both skip-count lists. Do not stop at the first five if they have not met yet."
- "Is that the smallest meeting, or just a later meeting?"
- "Could you add the two numbers instead? Why does that fail for 4 and 6?"

Family calendars (swim lesson every 3 days, library every 4 days) make the idea stick.

## Thinking Questions

1. Why is the LCM never smaller than the larger of the two numbers?
2. When is the LCM simply the product of the two numbers?
3. How are GCF and LCM partners — one keeps the shared bricks, the other keeps every brick?
