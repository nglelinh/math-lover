## Hook

Take 9 buttons. Can you arrange them into a perfect square — the same number of rows and columns?

Now try 8 buttons. Something always sticks out.

Why do some counts make a square, and others refuse?

## Core Idea

A **square number** is a whole number multiplied by itself.

$$1 = 1 \times 1 = 1^{2}$$

$$4 = 2 \times 2 = 2^{2}$$

$$9 = 3 \times 3 = 3^{2}$$

$$16 = 4 \times 4 = 4^{2}$$

$$25 = 5 \times 5 = 5^{2}$$

The little 2 means “use this number as a side twice.” It does *not* mean “write the digit 2 next to it.”

### Why the name “square”?

Because the dots form a real square:

```
1 (1×1):        4 (2×2):        9 (3×3):
●               ● ●             ● ● ●
                ● ●             ● ● ●
                                ● ● ●
```

If the rows and columns do not match, you get a rectangle that is not a square — or leftover dots.

### The first ten square numbers

$$1,\ 4,\ 9,\ 16,\ 25,\ 36,\ 49,\ 64,\ 81,\ 100$$

Notice the jumps:

$$4-1=3,\quad 9-4=5,\quad 16-9=7,\quad 25-16=9$$

Each next square grows by the next odd number. That is a pattern you can *see* when you add a new L-shaped border of dots around a smaller square.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Compute the next squares

Work these out by multiplying, then check with a drawing if you like:

- $$6^{2} = ?$$
- $$7^{2} = ?$$
- $$8^{2} = ?$$
- $$10^{2} = ?$$

### Activity 2: Build the squares

With buttons, tiles, or graph paper, build:

- 2 rows by 2 columns → 4
- 3 by 3 → 9
- 4 by 4 → 16

Ask: “If I add one more row *and* one more column, how many new dots appear?”

### Activity 3: Hunt from 1 to 100

Which numbers from 1 to 100 are square numbers?

Answer: $$1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100$$

There are only ten of them. That scarcity is part of what makes them special.

## Example Walkthrough

Is 36 a square number?

$$6 \times 6 = 36$$, so yes: $$36 = 6^{2}$$.

Is 50 a square number?

$$7 \times 7 = 49$$ and $$8 \times 8 = 64$$. 50 sits between them, so 50 is not a square number.

Is 81 a square number?

$$9 \times 9 = 81$$, so yes.

## Math Through Time

Pythagoras and his students, more than 2,000 years ago, loved numbers they could *draw*. Square numbers felt “complete” because the picture closed on all sides.

Babylonian surveyors used square numbers when they measured fields. A field that is 5 units by 5 units covers 25 square units — the same idea as $$5^{2}$$.

Today, square numbers hide in pixel screens, tiled floors, and chessboards (8 by 8 is 64).

## Parent Corner

The child is learning to connect a *picture* (a square of dots) with a *rule* (a number times itself).

Helpful prompts:

- "Can you build it?"
- "What is the next square after this one? How many new dots did you add?"
- "How do you know 50 is not a square without guessing forever?"

Play a “guess the side” game: you say 49, they say 7.

## Thinking Questions
