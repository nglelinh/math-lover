## Hook

You have 12 stickers. You want to share them into equal groups with none left over.

Which group sizes work? Which group sizes fail?

The answers hide two twin ideas: **factors** and **multiples**.

## Core Idea

### Factors

A **factor** of a number is a whole number that divides it exactly — no leftover.

Example: factors of 12

$$12 \div 1 = 12$$ → 1 is a factor of 12

$$12 \div 2 = 6$$ → 2 is a factor of 12

$$12 \div 3 = 4$$ → 3 is a factor of 12

$$12 \div 4 = 3$$ → 4 is a factor of 12

$$12 \div 5$$ leaves a remainder, so 5 is *not* a factor

$$12 \div 6 = 2$$ → 6 is a factor of 12

$$12 \div 12 = 1$$ → 12 is a factor of 12

**Factors of 12:** $$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$$

Factors often come in pairs that multiply back to the number:

$$1 \times 12 = 12,\quad 2 \times 6 = 12,\quad 3 \times 4 = 12$$

### Multiples

A **multiple** of a number is what you get when you multiply that number by 1, 2, 3, 4, ...

Example: multiples of 5

$$5 \times 1 = 5$$

$$5 \times 2 = 10$$

$$5 \times 3 = 15$$

$$5 \times 4 = 20$$

$$5 \times 5 = 25$$

**Multiples of 5:** $$5, 10, 15, 20, 25, \ldots$$

The list never ends. Factors of a number *do* end, because you cannot have a factor larger than the number itself (for positive whole numbers).

### How the two ideas shake hands

If 3 is a factor of 12, then 12 is a multiple of 3.

Same friendship, two directions.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Factor lists

Find every factor of:

- 6 → $$1, 2, 3, 6$$
- 8 → $$1, 2, 4, 8$$
- 10 → $$1, 2, 5, 10$$

### Activity 2: Multiple skip-counting

Write the first five multiples of:

- 3 → $$3, 6, 9, 12, 15$$
- 4 → $$4, 8, 12, 16, 20$$
- 7 → $$7, 14, 21, 28, 35$$

Circle 12 in both the 3-list and the 4-list. What does that tell you?

### Activity 3: Shared factors

Which numbers are factors of *both* 12 and 18?

- Factors of 12: $$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$$
- Factors of 18: $$1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$$
- Shared: $$1, 2, 3, 6$$

The largest shared factor will become tomorrow’s adventure.

## Example Walkthrough

Factors of 15: $$1, 3, 5, 15$$ because $$1 \times 15 = 15$$ and $$3 \times 5 = 15$$.

First five multiples of 6: $$6, 12, 18, 24, 30$$.

Is 4 a factor of 18? $$18 \div 4 = 4$$ remainder 2, so no.

## Math Through Time

Euclid, about 2,300 years ago, studied factors carefully. He wanted a reliable way to find the *largest* shared factor of two numbers — a method still used by computers.

Eratosthenes later used multiples as a “sieve”: crossing out multiples of 2, then 3, then 5, to hunt for primes. You will meet that sieve in the next lesson.

Architects still use factors when they want tiles or window panes that fit a wall with no leftover strip.

## Parent Corner

The child is learning two views of the same relationship: “this divides that” and “that is a skip-count of this.”

Helpful prompts:

- "Can you share these objects into that many equal groups?"
- "Is this a skip-count of 4? Prove it."
- "Did you check every pair, or only the small numbers?"

Kitchen sharing (crackers, grapes, napkins) is enough material for a whole practice session.

## Thinking Questions
