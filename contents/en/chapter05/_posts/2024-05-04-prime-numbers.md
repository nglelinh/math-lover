## Hook

Some numbers break into smaller equal pieces. 12 breaks into 2 and 6, or 3 and 4.

Some numbers refuse. 7 only breaks as $$1 \times 7$$.

Those stubborn numbers are the bricks the rest of arithmetic is built from.

## Discovery Puzzle

For each number, list every factor:

- 2
- 3
- 4
- 5
- 6
- 9

Which numbers have exactly two factors? Which have more? Which has only one?

Do not name them yet. Sort them into two piles: “exactly two factors” and “more than two factors.”

---

## Core Idea

A **prime number** is a whole number greater than 1 that has exactly two factors: 1 and itself.

- 2: factors 1 and 2 → **prime**
- 3: factors 1 and 3 → **prime**
- 4: factors 1, 2, and 4 → **not prime**
- 5: factors 1 and 5 → **prime**
- 6: factors 1, 2, 3, and 6 → **not prime**

Numbers greater than 1 that are not prime are called **composite**. They can be built by multiplying smaller whole numbers.

### Primes less than 20

$$2,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7,\ 11,\ 13,\ 17,\ 19$$

**Notice:** 2 is the only even prime. Every larger even number has 2 as a factor, so it has at least three factors.

**Notice:** 1 is not prime. It has only one factor: itself. Primes need *two* different factors.

### Why primes matter

Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes — like building a wall from bricks.

$$6 = 2 \times 3$$

$$12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3$$

$$30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$$

That writing is called **prime factorization**. Two children who break 12 apart in different orders still end with the same bricks.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Prime or not?

- 7: factors 1 and 7 → prime
- 9: factors 1, 3, 9 → not prime
- 11: factors 1 and 11 → prime

### Activity 2: Break into bricks

Write each number as a product of primes:

- $$15 = 3 \times 5$$
- $$18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3$$
- $$24 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$$

A factor tree helps: split, then split again, until every leaf is prime.

![A factor tree breaking 36 into primes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6c/Factor_tree_of_36.png)

*Image: Wikimedia Commons — math diagram*

### Activity 3: Hunt from 20 to 30

Which numbers from 20 to 30 are prime?

Check each one for leftover-free division by 2, 3, and 5.

Answer: 23 and 29.

---

## Example Walkthrough

Is 13 prime? The only whole-number pairs that multiply to 13 are $$1 \times 13$$. Yes.

Is 15 prime? $$3 \times 5 = 15$$, so no.

Is 17 prime? No leftover when you test 2, 3, and 5 as possible extra factors. Yes.

## Math Through Time

Euclid proved there is no “last prime.” However far you go, another prime is waiting. You do not need the formal proof yet — just the wonder: the brick pile never runs out.

Eratosthenes (about 276–194 BCE) invented the sieve: write the numbers, keep 2 and cross out its multiples, keep 3 and cross out its multiples, and so on. What survives is prime.

In modern computers, huge primes help lock messages so strangers cannot read them. A prime with more than 23 million digits was found in 2017 — far too long to write in this lesson.

## Parent Corner

The child is learning a classification skill: sort numbers by *how they split*.

Helpful prompts:

- "How many different factor pairs can you find?"
- "If it is even and bigger than 2, can it be prime?"
- "Show me the bricks for 24."

The sieve on paper (numbers 1–30) is a better activity than a long lecture.
