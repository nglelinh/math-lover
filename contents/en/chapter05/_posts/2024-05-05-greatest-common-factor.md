## Hook

Lan has 12 pens and 18 notebooks. She wants to make identical gift packs. Every pack should have the same number of pens and the same number of notebooks, and nothing should be left over.

What is the *largest* number of packs she can make?

That question is the heart of today’s idea.

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

## Discovery Puzzle

List every factor of 12. List every factor of 18.

Circle the numbers that appear in both lists.

Which circled number is the largest?

That largest shared factor is the most packs Lan can make if each pack gets the same mix.

---

## Core Idea

The **greatest common factor** (GCF) of two numbers is the largest whole number that divides *both* of them exactly.

(Some books say **greatest common divisor**, GCD. Same idea.)

### Method 1: List and choose

GCF of 12 and 18

**Step 1.** Factors of 12: $$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$$

Factors of 18: $$1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$$

**Step 2.** Shared factors: $$1, 2, 3, 6$$

**Step 3.** Largest shared factor:

$$\mathrm{GCF}(12, 18) = 6$$

So Lan can make 6 packs. Each pack gets $$12 \div 6 = 2$$ pens and $$18 \div 6 = 3$$ notebooks.

### Method 2: Shared prime bricks

**Step 1.** Break each number into primes.

$$12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3$$

$$18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3$$

**Step 2.** Keep only the primes that appear in *both* lists, using the smaller count for each.

Both have one 2 and one 3.

$$\mathrm{GCF} = 2 \times 3 = 6$$

Same answer, faster when the numbers grow.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Three quick GCFs

- 6 and 9 → GCF = 3
- 10 and 15 → GCF = 5
- 8 and 12 → GCF = 4

Check each one by listing factors. Do not skip the list on your first tries.

### Activity 2: Gift packs

Lan has 12 pens and 18 notebooks. What is the largest number of identical packs?

$$\mathrm{GCF}(12, 18) = 6$$

Each pack: 2 pens and 3 notebooks.

![Children planning a fair share](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Children_Learning_Together.jpg/960px-Children_Learning_Together.jpg)

### Activity 3: Fast bricks

Find GCF of 24 and 36.

$$24 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$$

$$36 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3$$

Shared: two 2s and one 3.

$$\mathrm{GCF} = 2 \times 2 \times 3 = 12$$

## Example Walkthrough

$$\mathrm{GCF}(8, 12)$$

Factors of 8: $$1, 2, 4, 8$$

Factors of 12: $$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$$

Shared: $$1, 2, 4$$. Largest: 4.

$$\mathrm{GCF}(15, 25)$$

Shared factors: 1 and 5. Largest: 5.

$$\mathrm{GCF}(10, 15) = 5$$

## Math Through Time

Euclid invented a clever shortcut: subtract (or later, divide) the smaller number from the larger, then repeat with the leftover. The last leftover that divides evenly is the GCF. Computers still use this **Euclidean algorithm**.

Builders used GCF thinking to choose tile sizes that cover two different wall lengths with the same tile.

Musicians meet the same idea when two rhythms “fit together” on the same beat.

## Challenge Ladder

Level 1: Find $$\mathrm{GCF}(8, 12)$$, $$\mathrm{GCF}(15, 25)$$, and $$\mathrm{GCF}(20, 30)$$.

Level 2: You have 14 candies and 21 cookies. What is the largest number of identical treat bags you can fill?

Level 3: The GCF of 10 and 15 is which of these: 5, 10, or 15? Why are the other two tempting but wrong?

Level 4: If the GCF of two numbers is 1, they are called *coprime* (or relatively prime). Find three pairs like that. What does “GCF is 1” tell you about sharing them into identical packs?

## Parent Corner

The child is learning to find the *largest fair grouping* that works for two amounts at once.

Helpful prompts:

- "List both factor families first. Then circle the twins."
- "Could there be a larger pack size? Test it."
- "What is left in each pack after you choose the GCF?"

Snack packing is the perfect home experiment.

## Thinking Questions

1. Why is the GCF never larger than the smaller of the two numbers?
2. If you pick a shared factor that is *not* the greatest, what still works — and what do you lose?
3. How can prime bricks give the same answer as listing, but with less writing?
