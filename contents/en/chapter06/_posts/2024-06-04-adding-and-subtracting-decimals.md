## Hook

At the market you buy 1.25 kg of tomatoes and 0.5 kg of cucumbers.

How much vegetable is that altogether?

If you add 125 and 5, you get a wild answer. The secret is not a new operation. It is lining up the *same places*.

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

- add decimals by lining up the points
- subtract decimals by lining up the points
- fill empty places with zeros when the decimal parts have different lengths

## Discovery Puzzle

Compute $$12.5 + 7.3$$ two ways:

1. Think in tenths: 125 tenths plus 73 tenths.
2. Write the numbers in columns with the points under each other.

Did both ways give $$19.8$$?

Now try $$8.5 - 3.25$$. What extra zero do you need?

---

## Core Idea

### Adding

**Step 1.** Write the numbers in a column so the decimal points sit in one straight line.

**Step 2.** Add as if they were whole numbers.

**Step 3.** Bring the point straight down into the answer.

$$12.5 + 7.3 = 19.8$$

### Subtracting

Same lining-up rule.

$$15.7 - 8.4 = 7.3$$

### When the decimal parts have different lengths

Give both numbers the same number of decimal places by writing extra zeros.

$$8.5 - 3.25$$

Write $$8.50$$, then subtract:

$$8.50 - 3.25 = 5.25$$

Those zeros do not change the amount. They only fill empty places so the columns stay honest.

- $$9.5 - 4.3 = 5.2$$

### Activity 3: A shopping story

Lan spends 12,500 dong on a notebook and 7,500 dong on a pen. How much altogether?

$$12500 + 7500 = 20000$$ dong.

## Example Walkthrough

$$4.5 + 3.2 = 7.7$$

$$15.8 + 7.9 = 23.7$$

$$8.25 + 6.75 = 15.00 = 15$$

$$9.8 - 4.5 = 5.3$$

$$12.3 - 7.85$$: write $$12.30 - 7.85 = 4.45$$

$$20 - 5.75$$: write $$20.00 - 5.75 = 14.25$$

$$5.3 + 2.75$$: write $$5.30 + 2.75 = 8.05$$, not 8.15 and not 7.05.

## Math Through Time

Chinese abacus users have added and subtracted decimal-like place values for centuries: each rod is a place, and you keep the places lined up.

In the 1600s, mechanical calculators were invented to do the same lining-up without losing a column.

Today the lining-up rule still saves shoppers, cooks, and builders.

## Parent Corner

The child is learning that decimal addition is *whole-number addition with honest columns*.

Helpful prompts:

- "Are the points stacked?"
- "Do you need a zero to fill a hole?"
- "Can you check by estimating? Is 8.5 minus 3.25 near 5?"

Adding two prices on a receipt is perfect practice.

## Thinking Questions

1. Why must the decimal points sit in a straight line?
2. When do extra zeros help, and when are they optional?
3. How can an estimate tell you that $$5.3 + 2.75 = 7.05$$ is too small?
