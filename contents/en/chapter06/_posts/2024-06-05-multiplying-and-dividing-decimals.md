## Hook

Four friends each get 2.5 slices of cake. How much cake is that?

If you multiply 25 by 4 you get 100. That cannot mean 100 cakes.

The digits are right. The point still has to find its home.

You already know how to add and subtract decimals and how to multiply whole numbers.

Today the new move is: compute with the digits, then place the point by *counting decimal places*.

## Discovery Puzzle

Think of 2.5 as 25 tenths.

Four groups of 25 tenths is 100 tenths.

100 tenths is how many ones?

Write the same story as $$2.5 \times 4$$.

## Core Idea

### Multiply a decimal by a whole number

**Step 1.** Multiply as if there were no point.

**Step 2.** Count the decimal digits in the factors.

**Step 3.** Place that many digits after the point in the product.

$$2.5 \times 3$$

$$25 \times 3 = 75$$

2.5 has one decimal digit, so the product is $$7.5$$.

Check: 2.5 is 25 tenths. $$25 \times 3 = 75$$ tenths = 7.5.

### Multiply by 10, 100, 1000

Slide the point to the **right** that many places.

$$3.5 \times 10 = 35$$

$$2.75 \times 100 = 275$$

$$0.125 \times 1000 = 125$$

Each slide to the right makes the number ten times larger.

### Divide a decimal by a whole number

$$7.5 \div 3$$

75 tenths shared among 3 is 25 tenths, so $$2.5$$.

You can also divide 75 by 3 and keep one decimal place.

### Divide by 10, 100, 1000

Slide the point to the **left** that many places.

$$35 \div 10 = 3.5$$

$$275 \div 100 = 2.75$$

$$125 \div 1000 = 0.125$$

$$3.5 \times 4 = 14$$

$$2.25 \times 8 = 18$$

$$1.5 \times 6 = 9$$

$$15.6 \div 3 = 5.2$$

$$24.8 \div 8 = 3.1$$

$$2.5 \times 100 = 250$$, not 25 (that would be $$2.5 \times 10$$) and not 2500 (that would be $$2.5 \times 1000$$).

## Math Through Time

Ancient Egyptian scribes often multiplied by doubling. That method still works for decimals if you keep track of the point.

Roman times left multiplication tables on tablets. The tables were for whole numbers. The *slide-the-point* trick is what lets those same tables serve 2.5 as well as 25.

Today cooks double a 1.25-cup recipe, shops multiply a unit price, and scientists slide points when they change units.

## Parent Corner

The child is learning that the digits of a product or quotient come from whole-number arithmetic; the point is placed by *place value*.

Helpful prompts:

- "How many digits sit after the point?"
- "Can you check by thinking in tenths?"
- "What happens if you slide the point the wrong way?"

Doubling a recipe or finding the cost of several identical items is enough practice.

## Thinking Questions

2. How is dividing by 10 the opposite of multiplying by 10?
3. If $$1.25 \times 8 = 10$$, what does that tell you about $$10 \div 8$$?
