## Hook

A map says $$1 : 100000$$. One centimeter on paper stands for a kilometer outside.

A television screen says $$16 : 9$$. The width and height grow together.

Both sentences are ratios. When two ratios name the *same relationship*, we have a **proportion**.

How can you be sure without tasting any fruit?

## Core Idea

### Ratio

The **ratio** of two quantities is the quotient of one divided by the other.

Write $$a : b$$ or $$a/b$$.

5 apples and 3 oranges: $$5 : 3$$

20 boys and 25 girls: $$20 : 25 = 4 : 5$$

### Proportion

A **proportion** is an equality of two ratios.

$$2 : 4 = 1 : 2$$ is a proportion.

$$3 : 6 = 5 : 10$$ is a proportion.

### The cross-multiply test

If $$a/b = c/d$$, then

$$a \times d = b \times c$$

Check $$2/4 = 1/2$$:

$$2 \times 2 = 4$$ and $$4 \times 1 = 4$$. Equal, so the proportion is true.

This is the “corners multiply equally” test. It is a way to be sure, not a magic spell.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Write the ratio

- 6 oranges and 9 tangerines: $$6 : 9 = 2 : 3$$
- 15 boys and 10 girls: $$15 : 10 = 3 : 2$$

### Activity 2: Test the proportion

- $$3 : 6$$ and $$1 : 2$$ → yes, because $$3 \times 2 = 6 \times 1$$
- $$4 : 5$$ and $$8 : 10$$ → yes, because $$4 \times 10 = 5 \times 8$$

### Activity 3: Scale a weight

3 apples weigh 600 g. What do 5 apples weigh if each apple is about the same?

$$600 \times 5/3 = 1000$$ g

The ratio “apples to grams” stays in proportion.

## Example Walkthrough

Write the ratio of 8 to 12, simplified: $$2 : 3$$.

Write the ratio of 15 to 20: $$3 : 4$$.

Is $$2 : 4 = 1 : 2$$? Yes.

Is $$3 : 5 = 6 : 10$$? Yes, because $$3 \times 10 = 5 \times 6$$.

$$4 : 8$$ simplifies to $$1 : 2$$, not $$1 : 4$$ and not $$2 : 1$$. Order matters.

## Math Through Time

**Pythagoras** (about 580–500 BCE) studied pleasing ratios in music and later inspired talk of a “golden ratio” in art.

**Fibonacci** (1170–1240) wrote about sequences whose neighboring terms get closer and closer to a fixed ratio.

Map scales, photo sizes, and recipes are modern cousins of the same idea: keep the relationship, change the size.

## Parent Corner

The child is learning that a proportion is *two ratios that tell the same story*.

Helpful prompts:

- "Can you simplify both sides first?"
- "Do the cross products match?"
- "If we triple one part, must we triple the other?"

Look at a map legend or a “mix 1 to 2” garden fertilizer label.

## Thinking Questions

3. When would a recipe *break* a proportion — for example, if you add a fixed pinch of salt?
