## Hook

A friend holds out a coin. “Heads I win, tails you win.”

Is that a fair game? How do you *know*, before anyone flips?

Chance is not a mystery. It is a number we can measure.

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

- explain what probability measures

## What We Already Know

You already know how to share fairly and how to write a fraction such as $$1/2$$.

A fair coin has two sides. A fair die has six faces. Today we turn those familiar objects into numbers that say “how likely.”

## Discovery Puzzle

Imagine a bag with 3 red marbles and 2 blue marbles. You pick one marble without looking.

Which color is more likely? Can you say *how much* more likely — not just “red feels luckier”?

Do not memorize a formula yet. Count the marbles. Count the red ones. Compare the two counts.

## Core Idea

**Probability** is a number that tells how likely an event is.

It lives on a line from 0 to 1:

- $$0$$ means the event cannot happen
- $$1$$ means the event must happen
- numbers in between mean “possible, but not sure”

$$P = \frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{number of possible outcomes}}$$

### A fair coin

Two faces: heads and tails.

$$P(\text{heads}) = \frac{1}{2}$$

That is the same as 50 out of 100, or 50%.

### A fair die

Six faces: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

$$P(\text{rolling a 1}) = \frac{1}{6}$$

$$P(\text{an even number}) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$

The even faces are 2, 4, and 6 — three favorable faces out of six.

### A bag of marbles

3 red and 2 blue. Five marbles in all.

$$P(\text{red}) = \frac{3}{5}$$

Red is more likely because more of the bag is red. The fraction *is* the reason.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Ten flips

Flip a coin 10 times. Record H or T each time.

Count the heads. Count the tails.

Did you get exactly 5 and 5? If not, did the coin become “unfair”? Talk about the difference between *one short experiment* and the *true chance* of $$1/2$$.

### Activity 2: Die questions

For one roll of a fair six-sided die, find:

- $$P(\text{a 6})$$
- $$P(\text{an odd number})$$
- $$P(\text{a number greater than 4})$$

Check: the last one should be $$2/6 = 1/3$$, because only 5 and 6 work.

### Activity 3: The red-blue bag

A box holds 3 red marbles and 2 blue marbles. You pick one.

$$P(\text{red}) = \frac{3}{5}$$

What would change the answer: adding more red marbles, or painting a blue marble red? Try both stories.

## Example Walkthrough

### Example 1

A deck has 52 cards and 4 aces. You draw one card.

$$P(\text{an ace}) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}$$

![The ace of spades, one of four aces in a deck](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/Ace_of_spades.svg/960px-Ace_of_spades.svg.png)

*Image: Wikimedia Commons — math diagram*

### Example 2

What is the probability that the sun will rise tomorrow morning?

This is treated as certain: $$P = 1$$.

### Example 3

What is the probability that a bird will perch on a cloud and stay there?

This cannot happen: $$P = 0$$.

## Challenge Ladder

Level 1: A fair coin. Find $$P(\text{tails})$$ and $$P(\text{heads or tails})$$.

Level 2: A fair die. Find $$P(\text{a 3})$$, $$P(\text{even})$$, $$P(\text{greater than 4})$$, and $$P(\text{less than 6})$$.

Level 3: A box has 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Find $$P(\text{red})$$ and $$P(\text{blue})$$.

Level 4: A box has 4 red, 3 blue, and 2 yellow marbles. Find $$P(\text{red})$$, $$P(\text{blue})$$, $$P(\text{white})$$, and $$P(\text{not yellow})$$.

## Parent Corner

This lesson treats chance as a *fair count*, not as luck or superstition.

Helpful prompts:

- "How many ways can this happen? How many ways can anything happen?"
- "If we tried this a hundred times, about how often would we see it?"
- "Is this closer to impossible, fifty-fifty, or certain?"

Play a short coin or die game and write the fraction *before* you play. Then compare the list of results with the fraction.

## Thinking Questions

1. You flip a coin 10 times and get heads every time. Should you bet on tails next? Why or why not?
2. Does $$1/2$$ mean “exactly once every two tries”? What happens in a short experiment?
3. Which game is easier to call fair — a coin or a die — and how would you prove it?
