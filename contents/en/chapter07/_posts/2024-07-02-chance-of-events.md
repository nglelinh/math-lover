## Hook

The weather app says “70% chance of rain.”

Does that mean it *will* rain? Does it mean you should leave the umbrella at home?

The number is a promise about *how often* rain happens in weather like today’s — not a yes-or-no command.

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

- measure the chance of an event with a fraction or a percent
- compare events that are certain, possible, or impossible
- connect probability to weather, games, and simple decisions
- notice why a short experiment can disagree with the true chance

## What We Already Know

You already know the chance rule:

$$P = \frac{\text{favorable outcomes}}{\text{possible outcomes}}$$

You also know fractions and simple percents. Today we use that rule on new stories — and listen for words such as *certain*, *likely*, *unlikely*, and *impossible*.

## Discovery Puzzle

A weather model looks at 10 days that looked like today. On 7 of those days, it rained.

What number should the forecast show?

If tomorrow stays dry, was the 70% “wrong”? Or can a true chance still miss on a single day?

---

## Core Idea

An **event** is something that might happen: “rain,” “roll a 3,” “draw a jack.”

**Probability** is the number that measures that event.

### From a fraction to a percent

A fair coin:

$$P(\text{heads}) = \frac{1}{2} = 50\%$$

A fair die:

$$P(\text{a 3}) = \frac{1}{6}$$

A 52-card deck with 4 aces:

$$P(\text{an ace}) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}$$

### Words that match the number

| Words | About this big | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impossible | $$0$$ | Roll a 7 on a standard die |
| Unlikely | near $$0$$ | Roll two sixes in a row |
| Even chance | $$1/2$$ | Fair coin lands heads |
| Likely | near $$1$$ | Not rolling a 6 |
| Certain | $$1$$ | Rolling a number from 1 to 6 |

### Why 10 flips are not a promise

The true chance of heads is $$1/2$$. Ten flips can still come up 7 heads. That does not rewrite the coin.

A longer experiment usually sits closer to the true fraction. One day of weather is like one flip: the 70% can be honest and still miss tomorrow.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Coin diary

Flip a coin 10 times. Tally heads and tails.

Write the *experimental* fraction (what you saw) next to the *true* chance $$1/2$$.

Are they equal? Should they be?

### Activity 2: Even and odd faces

Roll a die 12 times. Count even results and odd results.

The true chances are both $$1/2$$. Did your 12 rolls land exactly 6 and 6?

### Activity 3: The 3-and-2 bag

A box holds 3 red balls and 2 blue balls. You pick one at random.

$$P(\text{red}) = \frac{3}{5}$$

Now change the story: the box holds 4 red and 6 blue. What is $$P(\text{red})$$ now? Did “red” become more likely or less?

## Example Walkthrough

### Example 1

Roll a fair die. Find $$P(\text{a 5})$$.

One favorable face, six possible faces: $$1/6$$.

### Example 2

From a 52-card deck, find $$P(\text{a jack})$$.

There are 4 jacks.

$$P(\text{a jack}) = \frac{4}{52} = \frac{1}{13}$$

### Example 3

A box has 4 red balls and 6 blue balls. Find $$P(\text{red})$$.

$$P(\text{red}) = \frac{4}{10} = \frac{2}{5}$$

## Math Through Time

Chance became serious mathematics when people asked a gambling question: if a game stops early, how should the prize be split?

**Blaise Pascal** (1623–1662) and **Pierre de Fermat** wrote letters that turned “luck” into careful counting. **Pierre-Simon Laplace** (1749–1827) later called probability the number of favorable cases over the number of possible cases — the same idea you are using.

![Pascal’s triangle, a number pattern linked to counting chances](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4b/Pascal_triangle.svg/960px-Pascal_triangle.svg.png)

*Image: Wikimedia Commons — math diagram*

Today the same counting hides inside weather models, medical trials, and insurance. A “70% chance of rain” is a grandchild of those old letters.

## Challenge Ladder

Level 1: Roll a die. What is $$P(\text{a 5})$$? Draw one card. What is $$P(\text{a jack})$$?

Level 2: A box has 4 red and 6 blue balls. Choose $$P(\text{red})$$: $$4/10$$, $$6/10$$, or $$2/10$$.

Level 3: Invent three events from your day and place each on the line from 0 to 1. Explain the placement.

Level 4: A forecast says 70% rain. You pack no umbrella and stay dry. Write two sentences: one that calls the forecast “wrong,” and one that still trusts the 70%. Which sentence is fairer?

## Parent Corner

This lesson stretches probability from games into *decisions*. The child should hear that a percent is a long-run story, not a guarantee about tomorrow.

Helpful prompts:

- "Is that certain, likely, or just possible?"
- "What would we count if we wanted a fraction?"
- "If it does not rain, does that erase the 70%?"

Watch a forecast together and pack (or skip) the umbrella on purpose. The next day, talk about the difference between one outcome and the chance number.

## Thinking Questions

1. Why can a fair coin still give seven heads in ten flips?
2. When is “70% chance of rain” more useful than the word “maybe”?
3. Can two events both be possible and still have very different probabilities?
