## Hook

A scientist does not guess which color the class likes. A scientist *asks*, *writes*, and *looks*.

You can do the same with a notebook and a row of bars.

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

- collect simple data with a question and a tally
- organize the counts in a table
- draw a bar chart from that table
- answer “most,” “least,” and “how many in all?”

## What We Already Know

You already know how to count objects and sort them into groups. You have seen bar charts in the last lesson.

Today you *make* the numbers yourself — then turn them into a picture.

## Discovery Puzzle

Ask ten people: “What is your favorite color — red, blue, yellow, or purple?”

Before you ask, guess the winner.

After you ask, write the four counts. Did the winner match your guess? If not, what did the data know that your guess did not?

## Core Idea

**Data** are facts we collect on purpose.

### Step 1: Ask one clear question

“What is your favorite sport?” is clearer than “What do you like?”

### Step 2: Record in a table

| Sport | Number of children |
| --- | --- |
| Soccer | 10 |
| Basketball | 6 |
| Swimming | 4 |
| Badminton | 5 |

### Step 3: Draw bars

```
Soccer:      ██████████ (10)
Basketball:  ██████     (6)
Swimming:    ████       (4)
Badminton:   █████      (5)
```

Each block stands for one child. The picture and the table must tell the same story.

### Step 4: Ask the data

- **Total:** $$10 + 6 + 4 + 5 = 25$$ children
- **Most:** soccer (10)
- **Least:** swimming (4)

Those three sentences are already an analysis.

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Color survey

Ask 10 friends for a favorite color. Tally:

- Red: …
- Blue: …
- Yellow: …
- Purple: …

![Colorful buttons that can be sorted and counted](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Plastic_buttons_20190306.jpg/960px-Plastic_buttons_20190306.jpg)

*Image: Wikimedia Commons*

Buttons or crayons make a hands-on version of the same survey: sort, count, then draw.

### Activity 2: Turn tallies into bars

From your color table, draw a bar chart. Give it a title. Label each bar.

### Activity 3: Three questions

Using only your chart:

- Which color won?
- Which color came last?
- How many people did you ask?

If the last answer is not 10, a tally was lost — or someone was counted twice.

## Example Walkthrough

### Example 1

Class sizes: 3A has 30 children, 3B has 28, 3C has 32.

Most: 3C. Total: $$30 + 28 + 32 = 90$$.

### Example 2

Trees planted: Monday 5, Tuesday 8, Wednesday 6, Thursday 10.

The Thursday bar should be tallest. The Monday bar should be shortest.

### Example 3

What is a bar chart for?

It is for *comparing* several amounts at a glance — not for hiding a single lonely number.

## Math Through Time

**John Graunt** (1620–1674) studied lists of births and deaths in London. People called him a father of statistics because he treated those lists as a story you could count.

In later centuries, armies and cities used the same habit: count the planes, the grain, the sick — then plan. A class color survey is a tiny version of that habit.

## Challenge Ladder

Level 1: From the class-size example, which class is largest? How many children are there in all three classes?

Level 2: Draw a bar chart for the tree-planting week (5, 8, 6, 10).

Level 3: A chart is mainly used to:

- A. compare several amounts
- B. show only one number
- C. refuse comparisons

Pick A, B, or C and defend it with a sentence.

Level 4: Collect one home data set (hours of sleep, fruit in the bowl, or books on a shelf). Make a table and a bar chart. Write one surprise the picture showed you.

## Parent Corner

This lesson builds the scientist’s loop: *question → collect → picture → conclude*.

Helpful prompts:

- "What question are we asking?"
- "Did we count everyone once?"
- "What does the tallest bar claim?"

A family survey (favorite fruit, bedtime, weekend plans) is enough. Resist fixing the child’s guess. Let the table win the argument.

---

## Thinking Questions

1. Why write a table before drawing bars?
2. What can go wrong if two people get counted twice?
3. If your guess and the data disagree, which one should you trust — and why?
