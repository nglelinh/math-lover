## Hook

A page full of numbers is hard to hear. A picture of those numbers can shout.

Which sport is most popular? Which month sold more books? A chart answers before you finish adding.

![A bar chart used to compare amounts](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/49/Bar_chart.svg/960px-Bar_chart.svg.png)

*Image: Wikimedia Commons — math diagram*

---

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, learners can:

- read a simple bar chart, pie chart, and line chart
- say which picture fits comparison, parts of a whole, or change over time
- pull a fact from a table or a chart
- explain why a picture can be clearer than a list of numbers

---

## What We Already Know

You already know how to count and write a table. You can compare “more” and “less.”

Today those counts become *heights*, *slices*, and *paths*. The numbers do not change. The eyes get a shortcut.

## Discovery Puzzle

Four friends report their math scores:

| Friend | Score |
| --- | --- |
| An | 9 |
| Binh | 8 |
| Chi | 7 |
| Dung | 9 |

Without adding yet: who is highest? Who is lowest? How could a row of bars make the answer even faster?

## Core Idea

A **chart** is a picture of data.

### Bar chart — compare

Taller bar means larger number.

Favorite activities:

```
Soccer:   ████████ (8)
Swimming: ██████   (6)
Chess:    ████     (4)
Reading:  ███████  (7)
```

Soccer wins the comparison because its bar is tallest.

### Pie chart — parts of a whole

A pie chart is a circle cut into shares.

If a pie is cut into 4 equal slices, each slice is

$$\frac{1}{4} = 25\%$$

Use a pie when you care about *how the whole is split*.

![A pie chart showing shares of a whole](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a5/Pie_chart.svg/960px-Pie_chart.svg.png)

*Image: Wikimedia Commons — math diagram*

### Line chart — change over time

A line chart connects points in order.

Temperatures in a week:

- Monday: $$25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$$
- Tuesday: $$27^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$$
- Wednesday: $$30^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$$

The line climbs, so the days are getting warmer. Use a line when the story is “what happened next.”

![A line chart showing values changing over time](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Line_graph.svg/960px-Line_graph.svg.png)

*Image: Wikimedia Commons — math diagram*

## Guided Discovery Activities

### Activity 1: Read the score table

| Subject | Score |
| --- | --- |
| Math | 9 |
| Literature | 8 |
| English | 7 |
| Science | 8 |

Which subject is highest? How do you know without a bar chart? How would the bars look?

### Activity 2: Draw favorite colors

In one class:

- Red: 5 children
- Blue: 8 children
- Yellow: 3 children

Draw a bar chart. Then say which color would get the biggest pie slice.

### Activity 3: Book-sale months

- January: 100 books
- February: 150 books
- March: 120 books

Which month sold the most? Would you pick bars (to compare) or a line (to show the path through the year)? Either can work — explain your pick.

## Example Walkthrough

### Example 1

A bar labeled “Math” reaches 12. How many children chose math?

Read the height: 12 children.

### Example 2

Soccer has 10 votes. Swimming has 6. How many more chose soccer?

$$10 - 6 = 4$$

### Example 3

A survey asked 10 + 6 + 4 children. How many children were asked in all?

Add the bars: $$10 + 6 + 4 = 20$$.

## Challenge Ladder

Level 1: From the four-friend table, who has the highest score? Who has the lowest? What is the total of the four scores?

Level 2: Draw a bar chart for books read: An 5, Binh 8, Chi 6, Dung 4.

Level 3: Choose the best chart.

- Comparing amounts → bar, pie, or line?
- Showing percents of a whole → bar, pie, or line?
- Showing temperature each day → bar, pie, or line?

Level 4: Sketch two charts for the same car-sale numbers (January 100, February 150, March 120, April 180): one bar chart and one line chart. What does each picture make easy to see?

## Parent Corner

This lesson is about *reading a picture of numbers*, not about drawing a perfect poster.

Helpful prompts:

- "What is this chart trying to compare?"
- "Where is the biggest piece? How do you know?"
- "Would a list of numbers have been slower?"

Look at a weather graph or a simple news chart together. Ask the child to say one sentence the picture is claiming — then check the axis labels.

## Thinking Questions

1. Why use a chart instead of only saying the numbers out loud?
2. What job is a bar chart especially good at?
3. What makes a chart easy to read — title, labels, or honest bar heights?
